## Key findings
- Evaluates prevalence at 10-25% among adults, but only severely bothersome in 1-7%.
- Hearing loss is the strongest risk factor for bothersome tinnitus.
- A thorough audiologic evaluation is a crucial first step, particularly for unilateral tinnitus or tinnitus accompanied by hearing loss.
- Diagnostic imaging is generally not indicated unless the tinnitus is unilateral, pulsatile, or associated with focal neurological abnormalities/asymmetric hearing loss (these represent specific "red flags").
- Natural history shows potential for spontaneous improvement over time (e.g. 40% of mild cases and 20% of severe cases reported resolution at 5 years in one study).
- Medications (antidepressants, anxiolytics, anticonvulsants) and supplements (Ginkgo biloba, melatonin) do not have clinical evidence for efficacy and are not recommended for treating tinnitus directly.
- Treatments with clinical backing include Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), which has positive quality of life outcomes, and acoustic stimulation (hearing aids/sound generators), which reduces tinnitus awareness.

## Quotes worth preserving
> "Familiarity with the natural changes in tinnitus that occur over time is important for counseling patients on expectations for improvement." (pg. 1225)

> "No medications, supplements, or herbal remedies have been shown to substantially reduce the severity of tinnitus." (Key Clinical Points, pg. 1225)

> "Clinicians should not: Obtain imaging studies of the head and neck in patients with tinnitus specifically to evaluate the tinnitus, unless they have one or more of the following: tinnitus that is localized to one ear, pulsatile tinnitus, focal neurologic abnormalities, or asymmetric hearing loss." (Table 2, pg. 1229)

## My / our take
This is an incredibly important anchor text for setting clinical boundaries. By confirming that medications and supplements have no evidence, we validate a non-pharmacological, app-based approach (CBT and sound therapy) as the clinical gold standard. The explicit mention of spontaneous reduction makes a strong case that our app should manage patient expectations, acting as a supportive tool for natural habituation rather than a "cure". It also clearly defines our medical red flags (unilateral, pulsatile, vertigo/neurological signs, sudden hearing loss) which must be hardcoded into the app's safety protocol.
