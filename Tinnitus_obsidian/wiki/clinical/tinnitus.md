# Tinnitus (이명)

## TL;DR

Tinnitus is the perception of sound — ringing, buzzing, hissing, or other noise — with no external source. Global population surveys estimate it affects 10-25% of adults, and about 1 in 5 Korean adults. However, it is only severely bothersome in less than 10% of cases. Most cases are mild and self-limiting; a subset become chronic and significantly impair quality of life. The dominant current model locates the problem not in the ear but in maladaptive brain network activity that sustains the phantom sound.

## Definition & diagnostic criteria

**Dictionary definitions:**
- "The sensation of sound without external stimulation" (American National Standards Institute, 1969)
- "The conscious experience of sound that originates in the head" (McFadden, 1982)

**Clinical working definition (Pusan National University Hospital):** "외부에서 Sound Input 없이 인지되는 괴로운 잡음" — distressing noise perceived without an external sound input.

Note: Heller & Bergman (Ann Otol, 1953) placed normal-hearing, tinnitus-free individuals in a soundproofed chamber — 94% reported tinnitus within 5 minutes. This suggests that some spontaneous neural firing is universal; the clinical problem is when that firing becomes conscious, chronic, and distressing.

There is no universally agreed formal diagnostic criterion. Diagnosis is clinical, based on patient report of persistent phantom sound, supplemented by audiometric workup to identify treatable peripheral causes and characterize the tinnitus (pitch matching, loudness matching, minimal masking level).

## Classification

### Objective vs. Subjective

**Objective tinnitus** — a sound that can be heard or measured externally (rare, <1% of cases):
- *Pulsatile* — arterial, venous, or systemic vascular origin; rhythmic, synchronous with pulse. Requires medical workup to exclude serious vascular pathology.
- *Muscular* — myoclonus of palatal muscles, tensor tympani, or stapedius.
- *Other* — patulous Eustachian tube (autophony / breath-synchronous sound).

**Subjective tinnitus** — perceived only by the patient (vast majority of cases):
- *Otic* — cochlear or central auditory system origin (most tinnitus belongs here).
- *Somatic* — somatosensory–auditory interaction (e.g., jaw movement modulates tinnitus; common in TMJ disorder).

### Acute vs. Chronic

- **Acute** (<6 months): may resolve spontaneously; different management threshold.
- **Chronic** (≥6 months): less likely to resolve; habituation-focused management indicated.

## Mechanisms (current understanding)

### What is established

Peripheral auditory damage (noise exposure, age-related hearing loss, ototoxic drugs) causes **auditory deafferentation** — reduced input from cochlear hair cells to the central auditory pathway. The brain compensates with:

1. ↑ **Spontaneous firing rates** in the dorsal cochlear nucleus (DCN) and inferior colliculus (IC) — homeostatic plasticity to maintain baseline activity.
2. ↓ **Lateral inhibition** — reduced contrast processing in the auditory pathway.
3. ↑ **Central gain** — amplification of auditory signals centrally.
4. **Thalamocortical dysrhythmia** — pathological coupling of slow (Δ/θ) and fast (γ) oscillations at the level of the thalamus and primary auditory cortex.
5. **Tonotopic reorganization** — cortical frequency maps expand into the deafferented frequency region over time.

See [[wiki/clinical/central-gain]], [[wiki/clinical/thalamocortical-dysrhythmia]], [[wiki/clinical/tonotopic-reorganization]].

### What is hypothesized / emerging

The **Triple Network Model** (De Ridder et al., 2022) proposes that tinnitus persistence is maintained by non-auditory brain networks: the Salience Network (SN) tags tinnitus as an important signal; this drives the Central Executive Network (CEN) to attend to it, suppressing the Default Mode Network (DMN). In chronic tinnitus, this tagging becomes fixed, explaining why emotional distress and attentional capture sustain the percept independent of peripheral status. See [[wiki/clinical/triple-network-model]].

**Traditional vs. modern view:**

| Traditional | Modern |
|---|---|
| Peripheral auditory system problem | CNS network disease |
| Damage to inner ear / auditory nerve | Maladaptive neuroplasticity |
| Target: correct peripheral lesion | Target: modulate brain network activity |
| Tools: hearing aids, maskers, drugs | Tools: TRT, CBT, neuromodulation |

## Comorbidities

- [[wiki/clinical/hyperacusis]] — co-occurs frequently; shares central gain mechanism.
- Anxiety and depression — bidirectional: tinnitus distress drives anxiety; anxiety amplifies tinnitus salience via the SN.
- Hearing loss — most common underlying peripheral factor; not always present.
- TMJ disorder — linked to somatic subtype.
- Sleep disturbance — tinnitus loudness perceived as greater in quiet environments (night).

## Assessment

- Audiometry (pure-tone, speech) — establish hearing thresholds, identify peripheral loss.
- Tinnitus pitch matching, loudness matching, minimal masking level — characterize percept.
- [[wiki/clinical/thi]] (Tinnitus Handicap Inventory) — 25-item self-report; most common outcome measure in Korean clinical practice.
- [[wiki/clinical/tfi]] (Tinnitus Functional Index) — 25 items, 8 subscales; more responsive to treatment change. **Primary outcome instrument for the app.**
- [[wiki/clinical/tmi]] (Tinnitus Magnitude Index) — 3-item perception-only short form, validated in Korean ([[wiki/sources/hwang-bahng-2018-ktmi]]).
- VAS (Visual Analog Scale) — rapid loudness / distress rating; used in many rTMS studies.
- Audiogram is essential before any sound therapy to categorize patient (see [[wiki/clinical/sound-therapy]] categories 0–4).

## Standard interventions

- [[wiki/clinical/trt]] — Tinnitus Retraining Therapy; directive counseling + sound therapy; 12–24 months; primary evidence-based management for chronic subjective tinnitus.
- [[wiki/clinical/sound-therapy]] — component of TRT; also used independently.
- [[wiki/clinical/cbt]] — Cognitive Behavioral Therapy; targets emotional and attentional response; holds the highest level of clinical evidence for distress outcomes.
- [[wiki/clinical/rtms]] — repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation; moderate short-term evidence; long-term not statistically significant in meta-analysis.
- [[wiki/clinical/tdcs]] — transcranial direct current stimulation; low evidence; best evidence for combination with tRNS.
- [[wiki/clinical/vns-auricular]] — auricular vagus nerve stimulation; emerging; must be combined with sound therapy.
- Hearing aids — first-line when hearing loss is present; amplification reduces central gain by restoring afferent input.

## Open questions / contested claims

- **Paradigm validity:** The thalamocortical dysrhythmia / Triple Network model is influential but not universally accepted. Some researchers argue it over-unifies a heterogeneous condition.
- **Neuromodulation efficacy:** rTMS and tDCS show significant short-term effects in meta-analyses, but long-term (6-month) data does not reach statistical significance (He Z et al., Front Neurosci 2025). The field has not established durable neuromodulatory cure.
- **Neuromodulation alone vs. combined:** Whether neuromodulation adds benefit to TRT/CBT beyond TRT/CBT alone is not well-established in RCTs.
- **Subtype-specific treatments:** The field is moving toward biomarker-based patient stratification, but no validated stratification tool is in widespread clinical use (see BTA Tinnitus Cure Map; McFerran et al., Front Neurosci 2019).
- **Objective tinnitus vs. subjective tinnitus** treatment approaches are fundamentally different; conflating them is a common error.
