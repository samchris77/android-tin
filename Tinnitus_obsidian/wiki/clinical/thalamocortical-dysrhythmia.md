# Thalamocortical Dysrhythmia

Abnormal oscillatory coupling between slow thalamic rhythms (delta/theta, Δ/θ) and fast cortical gamma (γ) bursts, proposed as the electrophysiological signature of chronic subjective tinnitus and other phantom percepts.

## Mechanism

Under normal conditions, the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) gates sensory signals: only salient input passes through to the cortex. When auditory deafferentation reduces cochlear input, the thalamus loses its normal "drive." This causes:

1. Thalamic neurons shift into **burst mode** — generating spontaneous Δ/θ oscillations instead of the tonic firing of normal wakefulness.
2. These slow oscillations interact with cortical circuits to produce **γ-band bursts** at the edge of the deafferented frequency zone — the frequency that corresponds to the phantom sound.
3. The resulting Δ/θ–γ coupling is interpreted by the auditory cortex as a sound signal. The patient hears the tinnitus.

This model explains why tinnitus can persist even after the peripheral damage has stabilized — the pathology has migrated into central network dynamics.

## Relationship to central gain

Thalamocortical dysrhythmia and [[wiki/clinical/central-gain]] are related but distinct:
- Central gain is the amplification of auditory signals throughout the central pathway (brainstem upward).
- Thalamocortical dysrhythmia is the specific oscillatory mechanism at the thalamo-cortical interface that generates the phantom percept.
- Central gain sets the conditions (high spontaneous activity, low lateral inhibition) that make thalamocortical dysrhythmia more likely to emerge.

## Relationship to lateral inhibition

Reduced [[wiki/clinical/lateral-inhibition]] (implicit in [[wiki/clinical/tinnitus]] pathophysiology) means the auditory cortex cannot suppress spontaneous activity at the deafferented frequency region. This allows thalamocortical dysrhythmia to sustain without competitive suppression from adjacent frequency channels.

## Therapeutic implications

Thalamocortical dysrhythmia is the primary target of neuromodulation approaches:
- **[[wiki/clinical/rtms]]** at 1 Hz over the left primary auditory cortex or TPJ attempts to reduce cortical excitability and disrupt the γ burst component.
- **[[wiki/clinical/tdcs]]** with tRNS over the left temporal area uses stochastic resonance to desynchronize abnormal oscillations.
- **[[wiki/clinical/vns-auricular]]** paired with sound aims to disrupt DCN synchronization upstream, preventing the dysrhythmia from being reinforced.

**Conversely**, sound therapy in TRT works partly by reinforcing TRN-mediated gating: teaching the vmPFC to classify the tinnitus signal as low-salience strengthens the TRN → thalamic gate closes → thalamocortical signal is blocked. See [[wiki/clinical/trt]].

## Open questions / contested claims

- The dysrhythmia model (originally from Llinás et al. on neuropathic pain) is influential but not universally accepted for tinnitus specifically. Some argue it over-unifies a heterogeneous symptom.
- Whether different tinnitus subtypes show different oscillatory signatures, and whether matching treatment to signature improves outcomes, is an active area (BTA Cure Map; McFerran et al., 2019).
- EEG biomarkers of thalamocortical dysrhythmia are used in research protocols but not yet validated for clinical stratification.
