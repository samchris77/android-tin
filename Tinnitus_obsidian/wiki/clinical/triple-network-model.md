# Triple Network Model (Tinnitus)

A neuroscientific framework proposing that the persistence and distress of chronic tinnitus is maintained by dysfunction across three large-scale brain networks — the Salience Network (SN), the Central Executive Network (CEN), and the Default Mode Network (DMN) — rather than by ongoing activity in the auditory system alone.

Primary reference: De Ridder D, Vanneste S, Song JJ, Adhia D. "Tinnitus and the Triple Network Model: A Perspective." Clin Exp Otorhinolaryngol. 2022.

## The three networks

| Network | Key nodes | Normal function |
|---------|-----------|----------------|
| **SN** (Salience Network) | dACC, anterior insula | Detects salient stimuli; allocates attentional resources; decides what matters |
| **CEN** (Central Executive Network) | DLPFC, posterior parietal cortex | Working memory, goal-directed cognition, sustained attention |
| **DMN** (Default Mode Network) | mPFC, PCC, precuneus | Internal thought, mind-wandering, self-referential processing |

## How the model explains tinnitus

The SN acts as a switch: when it detects a salient signal, it activates the CEN (focus!) and suppresses the DMN (stop mind-wandering). In healthy individuals, tinnitus — if perceived at all — is quickly classified as unimportant and filtered out, allowing DMN restoration.

In chronic tinnitus, the SN becomes persistently hyperactive in response to the tinnitus signal and tags it as a high-priority threat. This locks the system into:

- **SN ↑↑** — permanently flags tinnitus as important.
- **CEN ↑** — keeps attention fixed on the tinnitus signal.
- **DMN ↓** — suppresses the resting state; the patient cannot "switch off" from the tinnitus.

This explains why tinnitus distress is out of proportion to the acoustic loudness of the signal (which is typically near threshold) and why emotional factors (anxiety, stress) worsen the experience.

## Network states across the course of tinnitus

| State | SN | CEN | DMN |
|-------|----|-----|-----|
| Normal | baseline | ↑ during tasks | ↑ at rest |
| Acute tinnitus | ↑ | ↑ sustained | suppressed |
| Chronic tinnitus | ↑↑ | partially ↑ | partially ↑ |
| Adapted (habituated) | ↓ sensitivity | ↑ during tasks | ↑ at rest |

## Therapeutic implications

- **TRT and CBT** work at the SN level: counseling and habituation training teach the brain to reclassify tinnitus as a low-salience signal. Once the SN stops flagging it, the CEN's attentional lock relaxes and DMN can recover → [[wiki/clinical/habituation]].
- **Neuromodulation** targets the network pathology directly:
  - tDCS to left DLPFC (cathodal/inhibitory) suppresses the overactive CEN distress component.
  - tDCS to right DLPFC (anodal/excitatory) strengthens attention regulation.
  - tRNS to left temporal cortex desynchronizes the auditory source signal.
- **Critical constraint:** Neuromodulation cannot relabel the tinnitus signal as low-salience by itself. The SN's evaluation of salience requires behavioral context (counseling, experience of the tinnitus as non-threatening) that only TRT/CBT can provide. This is why neuromodulation alone is ineffective. See [[wiki/clinical/rtms]], [[wiki/clinical/tdcs]].

## Open questions / contested claims

- The model is a framework/perspective, not a validated predictive tool. Evidence for the specific network dynamics in tinnitus patients is largely from small fMRI studies.
- Whether the model is specific to tinnitus or applies equally to other chronic sensory percepts (chronic pain, phantom limb) is not resolved — this is both its strength (mechanistic generalizability) and a weakness (may not identify tinnitus-specific targets).
- Evidence level is `low` — the framework is influential and coherent, but the primary citation (De Ridder et al., 2022) is a perspective piece, not a systematic review or RCT.
