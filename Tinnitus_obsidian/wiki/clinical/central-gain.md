# Central Gain

The central auditory system's compensatory up-regulation of neural amplification in response to reduced peripheral input (auditory deafferentation). Central gain is a key mechanism linking cochlear damage to the generation and maintenance of tinnitus.

## Mechanism

When cochlear hair cells are damaged or destroyed, afferent input to the central auditory pathway drops. The brain attempts to maintain baseline activity levels through homeostatic plasticity — effectively "turning up the volume" centrally to compensate for reduced peripheral signal. This manifests as:

- ↑ Spontaneous firing rates throughout the central auditory pathway (dorsal cochlear nucleus, inferior colliculus, primary auditory cortex).
- ↓ Lateral inhibition — reduced suppression of neighboring neurons, widening the activated frequency region.
- Increased neural synchronization across cells tuned to similar frequencies.

The net result is that the central system generates signal where there is no peripheral input — the phantom percept of tinnitus.

## Relationship to peripheral hearing loss

Central gain is driven by peripheral deafferentation, but the two are not perfectly correlated:
- Tinnitus can occur with minimal measured hearing loss (audiogram may appear normal in the conventional 250–8000 Hz range; high-frequency or hidden hearing loss may be present).
- Once established, central gain can persist even if peripheral damage is partially repaired (e.g., hearing aid use reduces central gain over time, but does not immediately eliminate tinnitus).

## Therapeutic implications

- **Hearing aids** — the most direct intervention: restoring peripheral input reduces the deafferentation drive, which over time can lower central gain. First-line when hearing loss is documented.
- **Sound therapy** ([[wiki/clinical/sound-therapy]]) — enriching the acoustic environment reduces the contrast between background noise and the elevated spontaneous activity, providing background neural input that partially offsets central gain effects.
- **[[wiki/clinical/rtms]] and [[wiki/clinical/tdcs]]** — target the cortical expression of central gain (excessive excitability in the auditory cortex) rather than the upstream homeostatic driver.

## Relationship to other concepts

- Central gain is upstream of [[wiki/clinical/thalamocortical-dysrhythmia]] — it creates the high-excitability cortical environment in which the Δ/θ–γ coupling pathology develops.
- It directly causes [[wiki/clinical/tonotopic-reorganization]] when sustained long-term: the expanded spontaneous activity in the deafferented frequency region drives cortical map changes.
- It is the mechanism underlying the **vicious cycle of tinnitus**: ↑central gain → stronger tinnitus percept → emotional distress → attention to tinnitus → further sensitization.

## Open questions / contested claims

- The exact contribution of central gain versus thalamocortical dysrhythmia to conscious tinnitus perception is not fully resolved.
- Whether restoring peripheral input (e.g., cochlear implant) reliably reduces central gain and eliminates tinnitus varies considerably across patients.
